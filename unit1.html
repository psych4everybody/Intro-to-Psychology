Section A · Guide 1.0

History and Perspectives

What are the different ways psychologists explain behavior?

What you need to be able to do

  • Match an explanation of behavior to the correct perspective.
  • Describe the early schools of thought, their key figures, and their main limitation.
  • Recognize a biopsychosocial explanation and say why a one- or two-level explanation is not one.

Early schools of thought

Psychology is the scientific study of behavior and mental processes. Before the modern field existed, several schools gave competing answers to the same question: what should psychology study, and how?

SchoolKey figuresCore argumentKey limitation
StructuralismWundt, TitchenerBreak conscious experience into its basic elements, using introspection: trained self-reports of immediate experience.Introspective reports are subjective and cannot be independently checked.
FunctionalismWilliam JamesAsk what mental processes do, not what they are made of: how do they help us adapt and survive? Influenced by Darwin.Less systematic than structuralism; its broad scope made it hard to test.
PsychoanalysisSigmund FreudBehavior is driven by unconscious motives, unresolved childhood conflicts, and repressed desires.Hard to falsify; relies heavily on interpreting clinical cases.
BehaviorismWatson, SkinnerStudy only observable behavior. Internal mental states are unscientific. All behavior is learned.Ignores internal thought, so it cannot explain language, memory, or thinking.
Gestalt psychologyWertheimer, Kohler, KoffkaPerception is holistic: the whole is greater than the sum of its parts.Describes more than it explains; mostly limited to perception.
Humanistic psychologyMaslow, RogersPeople have an inner drive toward growth and self-actualization. Stresses free will and personal experience.Ideas such as self-actualization are hard to define and measure.

Contemporary perspectives

Modern psychology accepts that no single perspective tells the whole story. These are the lenses psychologists use today.

PerspectiveCore focusExample explanation
BiologicalBrain structures, neurotransmitters, hormones, geneticsA student's anxiety is linked to an overactive amygdala and high cortisol.
EvolutionaryHow a behavior helped our ancestors survive and reproduceTeenage risk-taking once helped young people gain resources and status.
PsychodynamicUnconscious motives, early childhood, repressed conflictsTrouble forming close friendships comes from an unconscious fear of rejection rooted in early loss.
BehavioralLearned associations, reinforcement, punishmentA student stopped raising her hand because past attempts led to embarrassment.
CognitiveThought patterns, beliefs, interpretations, memoryA student underperforms because he believes asking for help is a sign of weakness.
HumanisticFree will, growth, self-actualization, unmet needsLow motivation reflects unmet needs for belonging and meaningful challenge.
Social-culturalCultural norms, group influence, social contextStudents from collectivist backgrounds do better in group tasks than in solo assessments.

The biopsychosocial model

The biopsychosocial model is not one more perspective. It is a framework for understanding behavior through several levels at once: biological factors (brain, genes), psychological factors (thoughts, emotions, beliefs), and social-cultural factors (relationships, norms, environment).

How to spot a biopsychosocial explanationLook for three different levels mentioned together: something physical or neurological, something about thinking or emotion, and something about social context or culture. If only one or two levels are there, it is not a biopsychosocial explanation.

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. Explain the same behavior, a student who avoids speaking in class, from the behavioral and the cognitive perspectives.

Behavioral: speaking up has been followed by embarrassment in the past, so the behavior has become less frequent. Cognitive: the student believes others will judge her answers, and that belief stops her speaking.

2. What is the key difference between structuralism and functionalism?

Structuralism asked what the mind is made of, breaking experience into basic elements. Functionalism asked what the mind does, and how mental processes help us adapt and survive.

3. Why is "her anxiety comes from high cortisol and a habit of catastrophic thinking" not a biopsychosocial explanation?

It has a biological level (cortisol) and a psychological level (catastrophic thinking) but no social-cultural level, such as family, friends, school, or culture.

Section B · Guide 1.1

Psychology as a Science

What makes psychological knowledge trustworthy?

What you need to be able to do

  • Recognize curiosity, skepticism, and humility in action.
  • Tell whether a hypothesis is falsifiable, and spot self-sealing claims.
  • Explain what operational definitions, peer review, and replication do, and what they cannot do.

The scientific attitude

  • Curiosity: real openness to asking questions and following the evidence wherever it leads.
  • Skepticism: demanding evidence before accepting a claim, including claims from authorities.
  • Humility: being willing to revise or drop conclusions when new evidence demands it.
Why all three matterSkepticism without humility becomes closed-mindedness. Curiosity without skepticism becomes believing anything. Together they produce knowledge that is open to new ideas but resistant to wishful thinking.

The scientific method

StepWhat happens
Observation or questionSomething noticed in the world prompts a question. This is the starting point, not a conclusion.
HypothesisA specific, testable prediction about how two or more variables are related. It must be falsifiable.
Operational definitionAbstract variables are turned into specific, measurable procedures before testing.
Data collectionInformation is gathered using the chosen research design.
Analysis and conclusionThe data are examined to see whether they support the hypothesis.
Peer reviewOther experts check the methods, analysis, and conclusions before publication.
ReplicationOther teams repeat the study. Consistent results across independent studies build confidence.

A theory is a broad, evidence-based framework that organizes observations and generates predictions. A hypothesis is one specific prediction tested in a study.

Falsifiability

A hypothesis is falsifiable if evidence could, in principle, show it to be wrong. That does not mean it is false. It means it makes a specific enough prediction that evidence could count against it.

Not falsifiableFalsifiable
"Students who truly care will always find a way to succeed.""Students who sleep eight or more hours before an exam will score higher on a 20-item recall test than students who sleep four or fewer."
"Deep down, every person wants to be understood.""Students who get written feedback will improve more on a second essay than students who get only a grade."
Watch out for self-sealing claimsSome claims are built to explain away any evidence against them, such as: "If the therapy worked, it worked. If it did not, the patient did not commit fully." No result could ever count against this, so it is not a scientific statement.

Operational definitions, peer review, and replication

An operational definition states exactly how an abstract idea will be measured, such as "academic stress" as a score on a validated questionnaire. It makes a study repeatable and its findings precise.

The trade-offEvery operational definition captures only part of a complex idea. Measuring stress with a questionnaire leaves out the body's stress response. That is normal; what matters is being clear about what the definition does and does not capture.
Peer reviewReplication
WhenBefore publicationAfter publication
WhoOther experts, checking the submitted studyIndependent research teams, repeating the study
What it doesFilters out obvious errors, unsupported claims, and gaps in logicBuilds confidence that a finding is reliable, not a fluke of one sample
What it cannot doGuarantee that a study is correctProve a finding with one repeat; consistent replication across teams is what counts

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. Rewrite "Exercise makes people happier" as a falsifiable hypothesis.

For example: "Students who do 30 minutes of exercise on five days a week for a month will score higher on a 10-item mood questionnaire than students who do no extra exercise." It names the variables, how they are measured, and a result that could turn out to be wrong.

2. What can peer review not do, and what is needed in addition?

Peer review cannot guarantee that a study is correct, because subtle errors can slip through. Findings also need to be replicated by independent teams.

3. A researcher measures "friendship quality" as the number of messages two friends send each day. Name one thing this definition might miss.

It might miss trust, support in hard times, or time spent together in person. Two close friends who rarely text would score low.

Section C · Guide 1.2

Cognitive Biases

How does everyday thinking mislead us?

What you need to be able to do

  • Recognize hindsight bias, confirmation bias, overconfidence bias, and illusory correlation in everyday thinking and in research.
  • Explain why each bias is hard to correct, and suggest a practical way to reduce it.

The four biases in Unit 1

Cognitive biases are predictable, systematic errors in thinking. They affect everyone, including researchers.

BiasDefinitionWhy it happens
Hindsight biasAfter learning an outcome, believing you could have predicted it all along.Memory of what you thought before is revised to fit the outcome.
Confirmation biasSeeking, noticing, and remembering information that supports what you already believe, and filtering out the rest.Attention and memory are not neutral: we notice what fits our beliefs more easily than what challenges them.
Overconfidence biasBeing more certain of your judgments than the evidence justifies.People overestimate what they know and underestimate how hard tasks are.
Illusory correlationSeeing a relationship between two things that does not exist, or is much weaker than it seems.Memorable pairings are remembered; times when nothing happened are forgotten.
Why biases matter for researchBiases do not disappear in a laboratory. Confirmation bias can lead researchers to design studies that favor their hypothesis, report only supportive findings, and read unclear data their way. Peer review, publishing hypotheses in advance, and replication exist partly to check these biases.

The planning fallacy: a closer look

The planning fallacy is the tendency to underestimate how long tasks will take, how much they will cost, and how much effort they will need, even when past experience shows the same mistake again and again. It is overconfidence applied to time and resources.

It persists because when a task overruns, people tend to blame the particular circumstances rather than their way of estimating, so the estimating method never changes.

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. Suggest one practical way to reduce the planning fallacy.

Base estimates on how long similar tasks actually took before, for example by timing tasks and checking the record, rather than on how long the task feels like it should take.

2. Why is confirmation bias especially hard to correct through personal experience alone?

Because the bias shapes which experiences we notice and remember in the first place, our experience itself seems to confirm the belief. It takes deliberate steps, such as looking for evidence against the belief or keeping systematic records, to get around it.

3. How are hindsight bias and overconfidence bias related, and how are they different?

Both involve too much certainty. Hindsight bias is certainty about the past after an outcome is known ("I knew it"). Overconfidence is too much certainty about current judgments or future predictions.

Section D · Guide 1.3

Research Methods

How do psychologists design studies?

What you need to be able to do

  • Identify the method used in a study and what it can and cannot conclude.
  • Identify the independent and dependent variables, the groups, and any confounding variable.
  • Tell random assignment from random selection, and single-blind from double-blind.

Experiments and non-experimental methods

The method decides what kind of conclusion is possible. The most important distinction is between studies that can show cause and effect and studies that cannot.

ExperimentNon-experimental
What the researcher doesManipulates at least one variable and measures its effect on anotherObserves, records, or surveys without manipulating anything
Cause and effect?Yes, if well designed with random assignmentNo. It describes patterns and relationships only
Key strengthAllows causal conclusionsOften more realistic, ethical, and practical, especially for sensitive topics
Key limitationArtificial settings; not always ethical or practicalCannot rule out third variables or tell which variable affects the other

Key experimental terms

TermDefinitionHow to spot it
Independent variable (IV)The variable the researcher deliberately changes.What did the researcher change or control?
Dependent variable (DV)The variable measured to see whether the change had an effect.What did the researcher measure?
Confounding variableAn uncontrolled variable that changes along with the IV and could explain the results.Did something else change at the same time as the IV?
Random assignmentUsing chance to place participants into conditions, so differences between people are spread across groups.Did chance decide each participant's group?
Control groupThe group that does not get the treatment, used as a baseline.Which group had the normal or absent condition?
Experimental groupThe group that gets the treatment being tested.Which group got the intervention?
Random assignment vs. random selectionRandom assignment decides which condition each participant goes into, which supports causal conclusions. Random selection decides who is in the study from the wider population, which supports generalizing the results. A study can have one, both, or neither.

Blinding procedures

ProcedureWho does not knowWhat it controls for
Single-blindParticipants do not know which condition they are inParticipants' expectations, including the placebo effect
Double-blindNeither participants nor the researchers working with them knowParticipants' expectations and experimenter bias at the same time

The placebo effect is a real change caused by a participant's belief that they are being treated, whether or not the treatment is active.

Non-experimental methods

MethodWhat it involvesKey strengthKey limitation
Naturalistic observationWatching behavior in a real setting without interveningBehavior reflects real conditionsCannot show cause; people may act differently if they notice the observer
Case studyAn in-depth study of one person or a small group, often over a long timeRich detail; useful for rare casesFindings may not apply to others
Correlational study or surveyMeasuring two or more variables to find relationships, without manipulating anythingPractical and ethical for many topics; large samplesCannot show cause; directionality and third variable problems
Meta-analysisCombining and analyzing results from many existing studiesA more reliable overall estimate; shows whether findings are consistentOnly as good as the studies it includes

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. Why does an experiment need a control group? What would be missing without one?

The control group shows what happens without the treatment. Without it, there is nothing to compare against, so any change in the experimental group could be due to time passing, practice, expectations, or other factors rather than the treatment itself.

2. Why would a researcher choose a case study even though its findings may not generalize?

Case studies give rich, detailed information and are useful for rare situations that could never be created on purpose or studied in large groups.

3. In your own words, what does random assignment do, and what does it not do?

It spreads differences between people evenly across groups by chance, so a difference in results can be put down to the IV. It does not make the results apply to a wider population; that needs random selection.

Section E · Guide 1.4

Correlation and Statistics

How do psychologists read and interpret data?

What you need to be able to do

  • Read the direction and strength of a correlation coefficient, and explain why it cannot show cause.
  • Choose and interpret the mean, median, and mode, and identify skew.
  • Interpret standard deviation, percentile rank, and regression toward the mean.

Reading correlation coefficients

A correlation coefficient (r) tells you two things: the direction of a relationship and its strength.

What r tells youHow to read it
DirectionPositive: as one variable rises, so does the other. Negative: as one rises, the other falls.
StrengthThe closer r is to 1 or -1, the stronger the relationship. Values near 0 mean a weak or no relationship.
What it does not tell youCause and effect. Even r = 0.99 does not mean one variable causes the other.
Strength guideWeak: 0.00 to 0.39. Moderate: 0.40 to 0.69. Strong: 0.70 to 1.00. These apply to negative values too: r = -0.81 is strong. The sign shows direction, not strength.
ProblemWhat it meansExample
Directionality problemWe cannot tell which variable affects the other, or whether it runs both ways.Does exercise raise self-esteem, or does high self-esteem lead people to exercise?
Third variable problemA third variable might explain both.Ice cream sales and drownings rise together because both go up in hot weather.

Measures of central tendency and skew

MeasureDefinitionWhen to use itLimitation
MeanThe average of all scoresRoughly symmetrical dataPulled toward extreme scores
MedianThe middle score when scores are in orderSkewed data, because extreme scores do not affect itIgnores the actual values of the other scores
ModeThe most frequent scoreThe most common response, including categoriesThere can be several modes; says nothing about spread
SkewWhat it looks likeMean and median
Positive skewTail to the right: most scores low, a few unusually highMean is higher than the median
Negative skewTail to the left: most scores high, a few unusually lowMean is lower than the median
Normal distributionSymmetrical bell curveMean, median, and mode about equal

Standard deviation, percentile rank, and regression toward the mean

ConceptWhat it means
Standard deviation (SD)How spread out scores are around the mean. A small SD means scores cluster tightly; a large SD means they are widely spread.
Percentile rankThe percentage of scores at or below a given score. It is not the percentage of questions answered correctly.
Regression toward the meanExtreme scores tend to be followed by scores closer to the person's average. It is a statistical tendency, not a psychological cause.

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. Put these in order from weakest to strongest relationship: r = +0.52, r = -0.75, r = +0.18.

+0.18 (weak), then +0.52 (moderate), then -0.75 (strong). The sign does not affect strength.

2. Most students on a quiz scored between 8 and 10 out of 10, but three scored 1. Which way is the data skewed, and which measure of center is best?

Negatively skewed, because the tail of low scores is on the left and pulls the mean down. The median is the best measure of center here.

3. Why is "the mode" the only sensible average for a survey of students' favorite subjects?

Subjects are categories, not numbers, so they cannot be added up (mean) or put in numerical order (median). The mode simply finds the most common answer.

Section F · Guide 1.5

Research Ethics

What responsibilities do researchers have toward their participants?

What you need to be able to do

  • Identify each ethical principle in a study description and apply it.
  • Explain when deception is allowed and why it must be followed by debriefing.
  • Explain the role of an IRB and when its review happens.

Core ethical principles

Research often asks people to share personal information, feel some discomfort, or be observed. Ethical guidelines protect participants while still allowing research that genuinely adds to understanding. They reflect a commitment to treating participants as people, not data.

PrincipleWhat it requiresImportant detail
Informed consentParticipants get enough information about the study and freely agree before it starts.No pressure or deception into agreeing, and they can withdraw at any time without penalty.
Informed assentParticipants under 18 give their own agreement as well as parental consent.Parental consent alone is not enough. Both are needed.
Protection from harmPhysical and psychological risks are kept to a minimum and must be worth the potential benefit.A study with no scientific value cannot justify any risk.
ConfidentialityData are stored and reported so participants cannot be identified.Removing a name is not enough if other details still point to one person.
DeceptionParticipants may be misled about the true purpose only when the question cannot be answered otherwise and the risk is minimal.Allowed only under these conditions, and always followed by debriefing.
DebriefingAfter taking part, participants are told the true purpose, any deception is explained, and they can ask questions or withdraw their data.Required when deception was used, and good practice in all research.
IRB reviewBefore any data are collected, a research plan must be reviewed by an Institutional Review Board (IRB), also called an ethics committee. It checks that participants will be protected. The review happens before the study, not after.
Why deception and debriefing go togetherDeception weakens informed consent by holding back the true purpose. Debriefing partly restores it afterward by telling participants everything and letting them withdraw. Deception without debriefing cannot be justified.

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. What is the difference between confidentiality and anonymity?

Confidentiality means the researcher knows who the participants are but keeps their identities protected. Anonymity is stronger: no identifying information is collected at all, so responses cannot be linked to anyone.

2. Why must deception always be followed by debriefing?

Deception means participants agreed without knowing the true purpose. Debriefing tells them everything afterward and lets them withdraw their data, which partly restores their informed consent.

3. Why does an IRB review happen before a study, not after?

Its job is to protect participants, so problems have to be caught before anyone takes part. Checking afterward could not prevent harm that had already happened.

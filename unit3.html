Section A

Personality Theory

What makes you distinctively you, and where did that come from?

What you need to be able to do

  • Name the four approaches to personality and give one strength and one limitation of each.
  • Decide which approach best explains a behavior in a scenario, and say what that approach misses.
  • Tell the six defense mechanisms apart in everyday examples.
  • Explain the difference between self-efficacy, locus of control, and explanatory style.

What personality is

Personality is a person's characteristic patterns of thinking, feeling, and behaving that stay relatively stable across time and situations. Four major traditions explain where these patterns come from. They do not always agree, but together they give a fuller picture than any one of them alone.

ApproachCore questionKey figures
PsychodynamicWhat unconscious forces shape who we are?Freud, Jung, Adler
HumanisticWhat does a person need to grow into their full potential?Maslow, Rogers
TraitWhat stable dimensions best describe how people differ?Allport, McCrae, Costa
Social-cognitiveHow do personality, behavior, and environment shape each other?Bandura, Mischel

The psychodynamic approach: Freud

StructureOperating principleWhat it does
IdPleasure principleThe primitive, unconscious source of basic drives. Wants immediate gratification. Present from birth, with no sense of consequences.
EgoReality principleThe largely conscious go-between for the id's demands and the real world. Finds realistic ways to meet needs.
SuperegoMoral principleValues absorbed from parents and society. The inner moral critic that produces guilt and shame.

When the id, the superego, or outside reality creates anxiety, the ego uses defense mechanisms: unconscious strategies that manage anxiety by distorting or denying a threatening reality. They are not deliberate. The person is usually unaware of using them.

MechanismWhat it involves
RepressionBlocking a threatening thought, memory, or desire from conscious awareness entirely.
ProjectionAttributing your own unacceptable impulses or feelings to someone else.
RationalizationBuilding a logical-sounding justification for a decision that was really driven by emotion.
DisplacementRedirecting an emotion from its original target to a safer substitute.
RegressionGoing back to an earlier, less mature pattern of behavior under stress.
DenialRefusing to acknowledge an uncomfortable reality, even when aware of it at some level.
Strength and limitation in one breathFreud gave psychology the idea that much of mental life runs below conscious awareness and that early experience shapes adult personality. The limitation is that core ideas such as the id and repression are hard to test scientifically, and much of the original evidence came from a small, unrepresentative group of clinical patients.

The humanistic approach: Maslow and Rogers

Humanistic psychology grew as a response to what its founders saw as the pessimism of psychoanalysis and the mechanical view of behaviorism. It puts human potential, growth, and self-determination at the center.

Maslow's hierarchy of needs

LevelNeedExamples
5 (top)Self-actualizationFulfilling your potential; living in line with your deepest values.
4EsteemAchievement, competence, recognition, respect.
3Belonging and loveFriendship, closeness, family, connection.
2SafetySecurity, stability, freedom from fear.
1 (base)PhysiologicalFood, water, shelter, sleep, warmth.
Motivational sequencingMaslow's key claim is that lower needs generally have to be met well enough before higher needs become strong motivators. He described the hierarchy as an order of priorities rather than a rigid pyramid, even though it is usually drawn as one.

Rogers and the self

Carl Rogers put the self at the center of his theory. Psychological well-being depends on congruence between a person's self-concept and their actual lived experience.

ConceptWhat it means
Self-conceptYour organized beliefs about who you are: your characteristics, values, and sense of self.
Ideal selfThe person you feel you should be or wish to be, shaped partly by standards you have absorbed.
CongruenceA match between self-concept and lived experience. Linked to psychological health.
IncongruenceA gap between self-concept and experience, often produced by rigid conditions of worth.
Conditions of worthThe conditions under which a person feels accepted. When rigid and imposed from outside, the person hides their real experience to keep approval.
Unconditional positive regardAcceptance that does not depend on meeting any standard. Rogers saw it as necessary for genuine growth.

Trait theory and the Big Five

Trait theories ask a more descriptive question: what are the stable dimensions along which people differ, and how can we measure them reliably? The Big Five came out of decades of research on the words people use to describe personality, not from one theorist's clinical observations.

DimensionHigh endLow end
OpennessImaginative, curious, drawn to new and complex things.Conventional, prefers the familiar, practical.
ConscientiousnessOrganized, dependable, self-disciplined, goal-directed.Flexible, spontaneous, less driven by long-term goals.
ExtraversionSociable, assertive, energetic, seeks social stimulation.Reserved, reflective, comfortable alone.
AgreeablenessCooperative, trusting, empathetic, values harmony.Competitive, skeptical, blunt, less focused on harmony.
NeuroticismTends toward negative emotion: anxiety, moodiness, reactivity.Emotionally stable, calm, resilient.
The most important caution about the Big FiveThe Big Five is a taxonomy, not a causal theory. It reliably describes and predicts outcomes such as academic performance, relationship quality, and job success, but it does not explain where these dimensions come from or how they develop. Predicting and explaining are not the same thing. For the why, you need the other three approaches. Traits are fairly stable across life, although conscientiousness and agreeableness tend to rise gradually in adulthood.

Social-cognitive theory: Bandura

Bandura argued that you cannot understand personality by looking at the person alone. Personality comes from the ongoing, two-way interaction between the person, their behavior, and their environment. He called this reciprocal determinism.

ElementWhat it includesHow it shapes the others
PersonBeliefs, expectations, thinking patterns, self-efficacy, emotional tendencies.Shapes which behaviors are chosen and how situations are interpreted.
BehaviorThe actions the person actually takes.Produces consequences in the environment and changes how the person sees themselves.
EnvironmentPhysical and social setting, other people's reactions, what the situation demands.Shapes the person's beliefs and decides the consequences of behavior.

The key idea is that influence runs in all three directions at once. No single element is simply the cause. A belief shapes behavior, which gets a reaction from the environment, which feeds back and changes the belief, and the cycle continues.

Self-efficacySelf-efficacy is your belief in your ability to succeed at a specific task. It is domain-specific, not general self-esteem: you can have high self-efficacy in writing and low self-efficacy in public speaking. It is built from four sources: mastery experiences (succeeding yourself), vicarious learning (watching someone similar succeed), verbal persuasion (encouragement from someone credible), and physiological states (how your body feels during the task). High self-efficacy tends to lead to approaching a task; low self-efficacy tends to lead to avoiding it, even when the skill is there.

Two related ideas

Locus of control is how much you believe outcomes are controlled by your own actions (internal) or by outside forces such as luck or other people (external). Explanatory style, which you met in Unit 2, is your usual way of explaining why good and bad things happen, from optimistic to pessimistic.

Keep the three apartSelf-efficacy is a belief about whether you can do a specific task. Locus of control is a belief about who or what controls outcomes in general. Explanatory style is how you explain events after they happen.

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. During exam week, a 17-year-old starts sleeping with a stuffed toy from early childhood and sulks when he is asked to help at home. Which defense mechanism does this best fit, and why?

Regression: under stress he goes back to an earlier, less mature pattern of behavior. Like all defense mechanisms, it is unconscious, so he is unlikely to see it as a strategy.

2. According to Rogers, what is the difference between a person's self-concept and their ideal self?

The self-concept is who you believe you are. The ideal self is who you feel you should be or wish to be. Rogers linked psychological health to congruence between the self-concept and actual experience; rigid conditions of worth can widen the gap.

3. Why is the Big Five described as a taxonomy rather than a causal theory?

It sorts and describes personality into five dimensions and predicts outcomes well, but it does not explain where those dimensions come from or how they develop. Explaining origins needs the psychodynamic, humanistic, or social-cognitive approaches.

4. Name the three elements of reciprocal determinism and explain why none of them is treated as the only cause.

Person, behavior, and environment. Each one influences the other two at the same time, so the cycle has no single starting point or single cause.

Section B

Measuring Personality

How do psychologists actually assess something as complex as personality?

What you need to be able to do

  • Name the assessment method used in a research scenario and give one limitation of it.
  • Name the specific bias operating in a scenario and explain how it distorts the results.
  • Explain why predicting an outcome is not the same as explaining it.

Five methods of assessment

Knowing that personality has stable dimensions is one thing. Measuring them accurately is much harder. Every method has its own strengths and limits, and every method is open to specific, named biases.

MethodHow it worksKey strengths and limits
Self-report inventoryPeople answer structured questions about their own thoughts, feelings, and behaviors.Quick and easy to give to many people. Limited by social desirability bias and by how well people know themselves.
Behavioral observationWatching actual behavior in natural or structured settings and coding it against set criteria.More objective than self-report. Slow, expensive, and limited to the situations observed.
Informant reportsPeople who know the participant well rate the participant's personality.Can capture what the participant cannot see or will not admit. Affected by the relationship between rater and participant.
Projective testsPeople respond to ambiguous images such as inkblots, and the answers are read as clues to unconscious concerns.Rooted in psychodynamic theory. Low reliability and questionable validity, but still used by some clinicians.
Semi-structured interviewsA flexible guide of open questions, with follow-ups based on the answers.Rich, detailed information. Open to interviewer bias and social desirability bias.

Five named biases

Knowing the name and the mechanism of each bias lets you spot it in a scenario, and in your own thinking.

BiasWhat it involves
Social desirability biasAnswering in ways that make you look good, whether or not the answers are accurate.
Confirmation biasNoticing and giving more weight to evidence that supports what you already believe, and downplaying evidence against it.
Interviewer biasThe interviewer's expectations, tone, or body language accidentally shape the participant's answers.
Participant biasInformants answer in ways shaped by their relationship with the person they are rating: loyalty, tact, or wanting to avoid conflict.
Observer effectPeople behave differently when they know they are being watched or studied.
The distinction tested most oftenParticipant bias applies to informants describing someone else, and comes from their relationship with that person. Social desirability bias applies to a person describing themselves, and comes from their own self-image. Both paint a more favorable picture, but the source of the distortion is different.
Why this matters beyond Unit 3These biases are not only about personality research. They apply to judging any psychological study, including the research methods from Unit 1. Spotting a bias in a personality scenario uses the same skill as evaluating a research design.
Predicting is not explainingA reliable link, such as conscientiousness scores predicting career satisfaction, tells you what tends to happen. On its own it does not explain the mechanism or show cause and effect. Keep this in mind whenever a question gives you a correlation or a prediction to evaluate.

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. Participant bias and social desirability bias both make people look better than they really are. Where does each one come from?

Social desirability bias comes from a person describing themselves and wanting to look good. Participant bias comes from an informant describing someone else, shaped by their relationship with that person.

2. A researcher worries that self-reports of agreeableness will be inflated. Which other method could she use, and what new bias might that method bring in?

Informant reports avoid self-presentation but can bring in participant bias. Behavioral observation is more objective but can bring in the observer effect, because people act differently when watched. Every method trades one weakness for another.

3. Why are projective tests still used by some clinicians despite their low reliability and questionable validity?

They come from the psychodynamic tradition, which holds that important concerns are unconscious and will not show up in direct questions. Some clinicians use them as a starting point for conversation rather than as a precise measurement.

4. What is the difference between predictive validity and explanatory power, and why does it matter when you read a personality study?

Predictive validity means a measure reliably forecasts an outcome. Explanatory power means it tells you why the outcome happens. A study can predict well without explaining anything, so a strong prediction is not proof of cause.

Section C

The Diathesis-Stress Model

When does a personality trait become a vulnerability?

What you need to be able to do

  • State the diathesis-stress model in your own words.
  • Find the diathesis and the stressor in a scenario you have not seen before, and set the symptoms aside.
  • Explain how the model fits inside the broader biopsychosocial view.

From trait to disorder

A personality trait is a stable, ordinary pattern. A psychological disorder is a pattern that causes clinically significant distress or impairment. The gap between the two is real, but it is not always wide. The biopsychosocial model says that biological, psychological, and social factors interact to produce or protect against a disorder. The diathesis-stress model is a more structured version of that idea: a disorder develops when two things are present together.

ComponentDefinition
DiathesisA pre-existing vulnerability, biological (such as a genetic predisposition) or psychological (such as a personality pattern or thinking style), that lowers the threshold for a disorder. It exists before any triggering event.
StressorAn event or ongoing condition that triggers the disorder in a vulnerable person. The same stressor may have no clinical effect on someone without the diathesis.
The most heavily tested skill in this unitFinding the diathesis and the stressor in an unseen scenario. The diathesis is the long-standing pattern described before the triggering event. The stressor is the specific event or change. A symptom, such as the worry, the lost sleep, or the withdrawal, is neither: it is the outcome the model explains. Do not assume neuroticism is the diathesis just because the disorder involves anxiety. The diathesis must be supported by evidence in the scenario.

Working through a scenario

  1. Find the one-time, specific event. That is almost always the stressor.
  2. Find the long-standing pattern described as existing before that event. That is the diathesis.
  3. Check whether any detail is actually a symptom (the outcome). Set those aside.
  4. Do not name a trait such as neuroticism unless the scenario gives evidence for it. Suppressed needs, perfectionism, or avoidance can each be the diathesis without neuroticism ever being mentioned.

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. A student has a long-standing pattern of perfectionistic, rigid standards. Three months after an injury ends her season, she begins compulsively reviewing every detail, searching for what she could have done differently. Identify the diathesis and the stressor.

Diathesis: her long-standing perfectionistic, rigid standards. Stressor: the injury that ended her season. The compulsive reviewing is a symptom, the outcome the model explains.

2. Why can a symptom never be the diathesis?

The diathesis exists before the stressor and helps cause the disorder. A symptom appears after the stressor as part of the disorder itself, so it is the result, not a cause.

Section D

GAD and OCD

When does worry, or a need for order, become a disorder?

What you need to be able to do

  • Match symptoms in a scenario to GAD or OCD using their core features and time criteria.
  • Explain why each disorder is more than the everyday use of the word.
  • Name the vulnerability factors linked to each.

Two anxiety-related disorders

GAD (Generalized Anxiety Disorder)OCD (Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder)
Core featureExcessive, hard-to-control worry across several areas of life.Intrusive obsessions, paired with compulsions done to reduce the anxiety they cause.
Time and impactAt least six months, more days than not.Takes more than an hour a day, or causes significant distress or impairment.
Key criteriaWorry across several areas (A), hard to control (B), three or more physical symptoms (C), impairment (D).Obsessions and/or compulsions, not caused by a substance or a medical condition.
Personality linkHigh neuroticism is the most consistent predictor. High conscientiousness can intensify the pattern.Very high conscientiousness (orderliness, dutifulness) with low openness to ambiguity.
Shared vulnerabilityIntolerance of uncertaintyIntolerance of uncertainty
The line between a preference and a disorderLiking a tidy desk and feeling a little annoyed by clutter, with no effect on daily life, does not meet OCD criteria. The line is obsessions or compulsions that take a lot of time or cause significant distress or impairment. OCD is also ego-dystonic: the person usually recognizes their thoughts as irrational but cannot stop them, which separates OCD from delusional thinking.

Thinking patterns linked to these disorders

Intolerance of uncertainty means finding not-knowing very hard to bear. It is a vulnerability shared by GAD and OCD: in GAD it feeds endless "what if" worry, and in OCD it feeds the urge to check or repeat until things feel certain.

Thought-action fusion is the belief that having a thought is as bad as acting on it, or makes the event more likely to happen. It is linked to OCD, because an unwanted thought then feels dangerous and has to be "undone" with a compulsion.

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. What specifically separates clinical OCD from a strong preference for order and tidiness?

OCD involves obsessions and/or compulsions that take over an hour a day or cause significant distress or impairment, and the person usually recognizes the thoughts as irrational (ego-dystonic). A preference with no effect on daily life does not meet that threshold.

2. What vulnerability factor do GAD and OCD share, and how does it show up differently in each?

Intolerance of uncertainty. In GAD it fuels constant worry about many possible outcomes. In OCD it fuels checking or repeating until things feel certain.

Section E

Major Depressive Disorder

What separates depression from ordinary sadness?

What you need to be able to do

  • Recognize the DSM-5 criteria and duration for MDD.
  • Explain anhedonia and why it matters more than low mood alone.
  • Explain why normal grief is not MDD on its own.

Not the same as sadness

Sadness is a normal, healthy response to loss. MDD is a lasting disturbance in mood and functioning that persists for weeks, and it often includes symptoms that have nothing to do with emotional pain, such as changes in sleep, appetite, and energy.

CriterionDescription
Criterion AFive or more symptoms during the same two-week period, at least one being depressed mood or anhedonia.
Criterion BClinically significant distress or difficulty functioning.
Criterion CNot caused by a substance or a medical condition.
DurationAt least two weeks, and a change from how the person functioned before.
Anhedonia is the key distinguishing featureAnhedonia is the loss of interest in, or capacity to feel pleasure from, activities a person used to enjoy. It is not simple disinterest: a person with anhedonia can take part in something they used to love and still feel nothing. This is the most clinically important feature separating MDD from ordinary low mood. High neuroticism is MDD's strongest personality predictor; high agreeableness combined with pushing down one's own needs can also raise vulnerability. Loss can trigger MDD in a vulnerable person, but normal bereavement alone does not meet the criteria.

A thinking pattern linked to depression

Beck's negative cognitive triad is a set of persistent negative views about the self ("I am a failure"), the world ("nothing goes right"), and the future ("it will never get better"). Beck linked this way of thinking to vulnerability to depression, which is why it can act as a psychological diathesis.

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. What is anhedonia, and why is it described as more than simple disinterest?

Anhedonia is the loss of the capacity for pleasure in activities a person used to enjoy. It is more than disinterest because the person can still take part and feel nothing, rather than simply choosing not to join in.

2. Why does normal grief after a loss not count as MDD on its own?

Grief is an expected response to loss. Loss can trigger MDD in a vulnerable person, but bereavement alone does not meet the criteria.

Section F

Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder

Why does the same event affect people so differently?

What you need to be able to do

  • Name the PTSD symptom clusters and match symptoms to each one.
  • State the duration criterion.
  • Explain why PTSD's personality links are more complex than those of GAD, OCD, and MDD.

The symptom clusters

Not everyone who goes through a trauma develops PTSD. It develops in some trauma survivors, and involves four symptom clusters that last beyond the immediate aftermath.

ClusterDescription
Criterion A: ExposureExperiencing or witnessing a traumatic event, learning that it happened to someone close, or repeated exposure through work, involving actual or threatened death, serious injury, or violence.
Criterion B: IntrusionRecurring distressing memories, nightmares, flashbacks, or strong body reactions to reminders.
Criterion C: AvoidanceAvoiding thoughts, feelings, people, places, or conversations linked to the trauma.
Criterion D: Negative cognition and moodDistorted blame, persistent negative beliefs, loss of interest, feeling cut off from others, being unable to feel positive emotions.
Criterion E: ArousalIrritability, being constantly on guard, a strong startle response, poor sleep, trouble concentrating.

Duration: more than one month.

Why PTSD's personality links are "more complex"For GAD, OCD, and MDD, one trait direction (usually high neuroticism) is the consistent risk factor. PTSD is less tidy. High neuroticism is a risk factor, but low neuroticism combined with avoidant coping can also raise risk, because pushing distress away can prevent the emotional processing needed to make sense of the event. High conscientiousness can protect or harm, depending on whether it shows up as problem-solving or as rigid suppression of emotion. A lack of social support after the event is one of the strongest risk factors of all.

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. Name the four PTSD symptom clusters that follow exposure to a trauma.

Intrusion, avoidance, negative changes in cognition and mood, and arousal.

2. Why are PTSD's personality links described as more complex than those of the other three disorders in this unit?

Both high neuroticism and low neuroticism combined with avoidant coping can raise risk, and conscientiousness can help or harm depending on how it shows up. For GAD, OCD, and MDD, one trait direction is the consistent risk factor.

Section A · Guide 2.1

Attribution and Person Perception

Why do we explain people's behavior the way we do?

What you need to be able to do

  • Decide whether an explanation is dispositional or situational.
  • Spot the fundamental attribution error, actor-observer bias, and self-serving bias, and tell them apart.
  • Recognize explanatory style, the mere exposure effect, self-fulfilling prophecies, social comparison, and relative deprivation in a scenario.

Dispositional or situational?

We rarely just watch behavior. We explain it. Something happens, and almost automatically we give it a cause. This is called attribution, and it always asks one question: is the behavior caused by something inside the person or something outside them?

TypeWhat it meansExample
Dispositional attributionExplaining behavior by something inside the person: personality, character, ability, or attitude."She failed because she is lazy."
Situational attributionExplaining behavior by outside circumstances: the environment, other people, luck, or context."She failed because the exam was badly written."

Three attribution biases

The fundamental attribution error (FAE)

The tendency to overestimate dispositional causes and underestimate situational causes when explaining other people's behavior. When we watch someone act, we assume it shows who they are rather than what their situation was. Lee Ross named it in 1977. In a classic study, participants read essays for or against a government, were told the writers had been assigned their side, and still rated the writers as truly holding those views.

Culture matters hereThe FAE is stronger in individualistic cultures, where the self is seen as independent and self-determining. It is weaker in collectivist cultures, where situational and relational forces are more naturally taken into account.

Actor-observer bias

When we explain our own behavior, we stress the situation. When we explain someone else's, we stress their disposition. Same kind of behavior, a different explanation depending on who is doing the explaining.

Self-serving bias

We credit our successes to internal factors (ability, effort) and blame our failures on external ones (bad luck, unfair conditions). It protects self-esteem and is normal, but it gets in the way of learning from mistakes.

Explanatory style

Martin Seligman found that people differ in how they usually explain bad events.

StyleWhat it looks like
OptimisticBad events explained as external (not my fault), specific (just this situation), and temporary (this will pass). Linked to resilience.
PessimisticBad events explained as internal (my fault), global (everything is like this), and permanent (it will always be this way). Linked to helplessness.

Person perception: how impressions form

ConceptWhat it means
Mere exposure effectSimply being exposed to something again and again makes us like it more, with no particular positive event needed.
Self-fulfilling prophecyExpecting someone to behave a certain way leads us to treat them in ways that make that behavior more likely.
Social comparisonJudging ourselves by comparing ourselves with others. Upward comparison (with someone doing better) can motivate or deflate. Downward comparison (with someone doing worse) can reassure or bring guilt.
Relative deprivationDissatisfaction that comes from comparing our situation with a reference group, not from our actual conditions.

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. Why is the fundamental attribution error weaker in collectivist cultures?

In collectivist cultures, people more naturally think about situations, roles, and relationships when explaining behavior, so they are less likely to put everything down to a person's character.

2. Describe an everyday self-fulfilling prophecy, step by step, from the expectation to the outcome.

For example: a team captain expects a new player to be weak, so passes to her less. With fewer chances she makes fewer plays and loses confidence, so she does look weak. The expectation changed the captain's behavior, and that behavior produced the expected result.

3. Give one possible effect of upward social comparison and one of downward social comparison.

Upward comparison (with someone doing better) can motivate you or make you feel worse. Downward comparison (with someone doing worse) can reassure you or make you feel guilty.

Section B · Guide 2.2

Attitudes, Stereotypes and Persuasion

How do attitudes form, resist change, and sometimes shift?

What you need to be able to do

  • Tell a stereotype, prejudice, and discrimination apart in a scenario.
  • Explain why attitudes resist change (belief perseverance, confirmation bias) and how they can change (cognitive dissonance).
  • Predict how persuasion and compliance techniques work, and how long the change will last.

Part 1: Stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination

In everyday speech these words get mixed up, but in psychology they are three different things: a belief, a feeling, and an action.

TermTypeWhat it means
StereotypeCognitive (a belief)A generalized belief about a group of people, not necessarily accurate or applied to any one person yet.
PrejudiceAffective (a feeling)An unjustified attitude, usually negative, toward a group or one of its members.
DiscriminationBehavioral (an action)Treating someone differently, usually unfairly, because of their group membership.

A person can hold a stereotype without feeling prejudice, and can feel prejudice without acting on it. The three can occur together, but they are separate ideas.

Implicit attitudesAn implicit attitude works below conscious awareness and can shape behavior even when a person sincerely believes they hold no such attitude. Awareness alone does not guarantee control, especially under time pressure or stress.
Related biasWhat it means
Just-world phenomenonThe belief that the world is fair and people get what they deserve, which can lead to blaming victims. It is about deservingness, which makes it different from the FAE.
Out-group homogeneity biasSeeing members of another group as more alike than they really are.
In-group biasFavoring members of your own group in judgments, resources, and explanations.
EthnocentrismJudging other cultures by the standards of your own and concluding that your own way is better or more natural.

Part 2: Attitude change

Belief perseverance and confirmation bias

Belief perseverance is keeping a belief even after clear contradicting evidence, often by reframing the evidence instead of updating the belief. Confirmation bias is searching for, interpreting, and remembering information in ways that fit what you already believe. Belief perseverance needs contradicting evidence to have been presented; confirmation bias does not.

Cognitive dissonance

Cognitive dissonance is the discomfort of holding two inconsistent beliefs, or of acting against a belief. Festinger and Carlsmith (1959) showed that participants paid $1 to tell someone a boring task was enjoyable later rated it as more enjoyable than participants paid $20.

Why the $1 group changed their attitudeThe $1 group did not have enough outside justification for saying something untrue, so they reduced the dissonance by changing their attitude toward the task. The $20 group had a clear outside reason, the money, so they felt no dissonance and did not need to change. This is the insufficient justification effect.

Part 3: Persuasion and compliance

The elaboration likelihood model (ELM)

RouteHow it worksHow long the change lasts
Central routeCareful, motivated evaluation of the quality of the arguments and evidence.Longer, because it rests on the person's own reasoning.
Peripheral routeChange based on surface cues: attractiveness, popularity, source credibility, social proof.Shorter, because it does not rest on the person's own evaluation.

Three compliance techniques

TechniqueSequenceWhy it works
Foot-in-the-doorSmall request first, then a larger one.Consistency: agreeing once creates pressure to stay consistent with that self-image.
Door-in-the-faceLarge request first (expected to be refused), then a smaller one.Contrast and reciprocity: the smaller request looks reasonable, and the asker seems to have made a concession.
Low-ballAgreement on one set of terms, then extra costs revealed.Commitment: backing out after agreeing feels more costly than accepting the new terms.

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. Explain how someone could hold a stereotype without prejudice, and feel prejudice without discriminating.

A stereotype is only a belief, so someone can believe a generalization about a group without disliking it. Prejudice is a feeling, so someone can dislike a group but still treat its members fairly, in which case there is no discriminatory action.

2. A student believes "I care about my health" but skips breakfast every day. Name two ways she could reduce the dissonance.

She could change her behavior (start eating breakfast), change or soften the belief ("breakfast is not that important"), or justify the behavior ("I eat a big lunch, so it balances out").

3. What is the difference between in-group bias and ethnocentrism?

In-group bias is favoring your own group over others, for any kind of group. Ethnocentrism is specifically about culture: judging other cultures by your own culture's standards and seeing your own as better or more natural.

Section C · Guide 2.3

Conformity and Obedience

Why do people change their behavior because of other people?

What you need to be able to do

  • Tell conformity from obedience, and normative from informational social influence.
  • Describe Asch's and Milgram's findings and the factors that changed them.
  • Evaluate a classic study by explaining what its design does and does not let us conclude.

Two kinds of social influence

This section covers conformity, where people align with a peer group, and obedience, where people follow a direct command from a recognized authority.

TypeWhat drives itPrivate belief?
Normative social influenceWanting approval or fearing rejection.Often unchanged. The person goes along in public without agreeing in private.
Informational social influenceGenuine uncertainty: using others as a source of accurate information, especially when the situation is unclear.Often changed. The person comes to believe the group is right.

Asch's line studies

Solomon Asch found that participants gave wrong answers on about 37% of trials when a unanimous group of confederates answered wrongly first, even though the right answer was obvious. Interviews afterward showed most participants knew the right answer but went along anyway. Because the task was not genuinely unclear, this was normative, not informational, influence.

What broke the conformityWhen even one confederate gave the right answer, conformity dropped sharply. The pressure depends on the group looking unanimous. One dissenting voice makes resisting feel possible.

Milgram's obedience studies

Stanley Milgram found that most participants would give what they believed were dangerous electric shocks to another person simply because an experimenter told them to continue. Several situational factors raised or lowered obedience.

FactorEffect on obedience
The authority figure's physical presenceHigher when the experimenter was in the room; lower when instructions came by phone.
How legitimate the institution seemedHigher at a prestigious university; lower in a rented office building.
Distance from the victimHigher when the learner was in another room; lower when the learner was in the same room.
Gradual increasesSmall step-by-step increases produced more obedience than big jumps, through consistency pressure.
A defiant peerA confederate who refused to continue cut obedience from about 65% to around 10%.
The core argument Milgram was makingHis most important finding was not that people obeyed, but that obedience changed so much with the situation. That challenges the idea that obedience is a fixed personality trait. Obedience was never universal, though: a minority always refused, which suggests individual moral choice still plays a part.
How to evaluate a classic studySay what the design lets you conclude and what it does not, consider other explanations for the same result, and weigh any ethical concerns against the value of what was learned. Do not just list limitations: explain why each one matters for a specific conclusion.

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. Give an everyday example of obedience that is not harmful, and explain why it is obedience rather than conformity.

For example, leaving the pool when a lifeguard blows the whistle and tells everyone to get out. It is obedience because a recognized authority gave a direct command. Conformity would be getting out only because everyone else did.

2. What is the difference between going along in public and changing your mind in private, and which type of social influence goes with each?

Going along in public without agreeing in private goes with normative influence (wanting approval). Genuinely changing your mind goes with informational influence (believing the group knows better).

3. Write one evaluation sentence about Asch's study that explains why a limitation matters, rather than just naming it.

For example: "Asch's participants judged simple lines among strangers, so the study may overstate or understate conformity in real decisions among friends, where people care more about the outcome and the relationships."

Section D · Guide 2.4

Prosocial Behavior

Why do people help, and why do they sometimes fail to?

What you need to be able to do

  • Explain why people help or fail to help in a given situation.
  • Tell diffusion of responsibility from pluralistic ignorance.
  • Compare the three explanations for why people help.

The bystander effect: two mechanisms

Prosocial behavior is voluntary behavior meant to benefit others. Darley and Latane found that people who believed they were the only witness to an emergency helped much more often than people who believed others were also there. This is the bystander effect, and two different mechanisms explain it.

MechanismWhat it involvesKey feature
Diffusion of responsibilityEach person feels less personally obliged to act because responsibility feels shared.Needs no uncertainty about whether help is needed. Works through the number of people present.
Pluralistic ignoranceEach person looks to others to judge how serious things are, reads their calm as a sign nothing is wrong, and does the same.Needs a situation that is unclear, or seems unclear. Works through misreading other people.
They can work at the same timeIn a real emergency, a bystander might read others' calm as a sign it is not serious and also assume someone closer or better qualified will step in if it is.
Knowing about the effect can reduce itTeaching people about the bystander effect, along with specific strategies such as speaking directly to one named person instead of a crowd, can reduce its influence. Psychology can be used to change outcomes, not just describe them.

Three explanations for why people help

ExplanationCore claim
Social exchange theoryHelping, even when it looks selfless, is driven by an often unconscious weighing of costs and benefits. Helpers gain something: approval, less guilt, or a good feeling.
Social responsibility normAn internalized sense that we should help those who depend on us, felt as a real motivation rather than a calculation.
Norm of reciprocityAn internalized expectation that favors should be returned, based on past or expected exchanges.

These do not always conflict. Social exchange theory can explain why helping became reinforced over time, while the social responsibility norm describes the obligation a person feels in the moment.

Altruism is the narrower case: helping out of genuine concern for someone else, with no personal benefit expected.

Common mix-ups

Check yourself

1. You fall and hurt your ankle in a busy mall. Based on the research, what is the best way to ask for help, and which mechanism does it work against?

Point to one specific person and ask them directly ("You in the blue jacket, please call security"). This puts the responsibility on one person, which works against diffusion of responsibility, and makes it clear help is needed, which works against pluralistic ignorance.

2. A company donates to charity so its name goes on a building. Is that prosocial, altruistic, or both?

Prosocial, because it is voluntary and benefits others. Not clearly altruistic, because the company expects a benefit (publicity), and altruism needs helping without expecting anything back.

3. In your own words, what is the difference between the social responsibility norm and the norm of reciprocity?

The social responsibility norm is a sense that we should help people who depend on us, whether or not they can repay us. The norm of reciprocity is about returning favors, based on a past or expected exchange.
